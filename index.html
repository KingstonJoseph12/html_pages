SECTION 1 OF 11
OPENING ASSESSMENT

The Verdict, Up Front

A credible problem — not yet a venture-scale business.

I read the entire document — all 2,533 lines — and checked the material technical and competitive claims against current primary sources.

My conclusion: the document identifies a credible problem, but it does not establish a venture-scale business. Its biggest unsupported leap is from "customers may buy a useful assessment" to "customers will continuously use a workload control platform." The final recommendation becomes more confident after the founder expresses a preference for building a platform, without introducing new customer evidence.

The strongest opportunity is to prove and govern economically worthwhile changes to data workloads. Whether that supports a durable platform depends on the frequency of those changes, customers' willingness to implement them, and savings that actually reach their budgets.

All proposed prices, savings examples, and validation thresholds in this memo are hypotheses or illustrations — not observed customer results.

SECTION 1

The 10 Most Important Insights

Ranked by what breaks the thesis if wrong.

01Attributed spend is not removable spend.HIGH CONFIDENCE

A workload assigned $300K of warehouse cost may release little cash when removed. Rank opportunities by the resulting change in billed capacity and contractual obligations — otherwise the product manufactures savings that finance cannot capture.

Would be invalidated by: repeated removal experiments showing attributed cost closely predicts invoice reductions across a narrowly selected customer segment.

02The useful unit of migration is a bounded pipeline — with dependencies, owner, and service requirements attached.HIGH CONFIDENCE

An expensive query is not necessarily independently movable. Moving it may require copying inputs, maintaining freshness, and returning outputs to Snowflake. Those boundaries determine the economics.

Would be invalidated by: most valuable candidates proving independently executable with negligible input/output and governance dependencies.

03The benchmark to beat is a reasonably optimized Snowflake implementation.HIGH CONFIDENCE

Deleting unnecessary work, changing schedules, improving SQL, resizing warehouses, and negotiating contracts may outperform relocation with less effort. Migration savings must be incremental to feasible alternatives.

Would be invalidated by: customers consistently demonstrating large additional savings from offloading after bounded native optimization.

04Iceberg reduces one form of lock-in; it does not create workload portability by itself.HIGH CONFIDENCE

Storage compatibility helps, but SQL semantics, policies, catalogs, orchestration, writes, and downstream dependencies remain. Sell a proven workload outcome, not an open-format conversion.

Would be invalidated by: a representative production estate moving across engines with negligible changes beyond configuring shared storage.

05Recurrence must come from an actual operational workflow.MEDIUM CONFIDENCE

Price changes and occasional renewals do not automatically create subscription demand. New pipeline launches, material code changes, and regression investigations offer a more credible recurring workflow.

Would be invalidated by: customers repeatedly buying continuous placement monitoring and acting on it without integrating the product into development or operational change processes.

06Target readiness matters more than raw Snowflake spend.MEDIUM CONFIDENCE

A $2M customer already operating AWS analytics may be much easier to serve than a $10M customer requiring a new operating model. Qualify for an approved destination, an accountable owner, and a practical budget reduction.

Would be invalidated by: customers without an existing target platform adopting and operating one quickly enough that onboarding and support remain economical.

07Benchmarking is the product's evidence mechanism — not a feature to add later.HIGH CONFIDENCE

Metadata can identify candidates; it cannot establish target performance and TCO. A narrow benchmark must arrive before a broad dashboard.

Would be invalidated by: predictions from metadata alone achieving sufficiently reliable out-of-customer accuracy that buyers confidently implement recommendations without benchmark evidence.

08Neutrality is a credibility advantage, not a moat.HIGH CONFIDENCE

Established FinOps vendors can compare alternatives too, and they have distribution and access. The real advantage must be faster, more reliable decisions and execution for a specific workload class.

Would be invalidated by: buyers systematically rejecting vendor-affiliated alternatives and paying a durable premium for independence alone.

09A paid assessment validates diagnosis demand; an implemented change validates the underlying company thesis.HIGH CONFIDENCE

Customers may buy reports for negotiations, internal politics, or architecture reviews. Require evidence of engineering allocation and implementation intent before believing the deeper thesis.

Would be invalidated by: assessments alone generating sufficient repeat purchases, retention, margins, and acquisition efficiency to sustain the intended business.

10Snowflake offloading is a beachhead with an unproven expansion path.HIGH CONFIDENCE

Large warehouse bills are not the software TAM. Enough reachable buyers, recurring value, and repeatable delivery are needed before adding platforms can create venture scale.

Would be invalidated by: bottom-up customer counts, paid conversion, retention, and account expansion demonstrating a sufficiently large software business inside this niche alone.

Worth dwelling on — Insight 1. Snowflake's query attribution excludes idle warehouse time and several other cost categories; it is an allocation of resource consumption, not a prediction of what disappears after an intervention. Snowflake query attribution documentation ↗
SECTION 2

Facts vs. Hypotheses

What's actually established, what's plausible, and what's speculation that shouldn't drive the product yet.

Reasonably established facts

  • Substantial enterprise spend exists to investigate. Snowflake reported 828 customers generating more than $1M in trailing annual product revenue in Q2 FY2027. This establishes spending capacity — not dissatisfaction or migration demand. Snowflake results ↗
  • Useful discovery telemetry exists. Query history, warehouse metering, and access history support substantial automated analysis. Access history requires Enterprise Edition or higher; available metadata and permissions vary. Query history ↗, Access history ↗
  • Existing Snowflake tables do not become Iceberg tables merely by changing the default table format. Snowflake format configuration ↗
  • Open table access does not automatically preserve fine-grained policies. Snowflake documents restrictions on direct external-engine access to tables protected by masking or row-access policies. Horizon access documentation ↗
  • Cost visibility and automated optimization already have competitors. SELECT advertises both. The competitive landscape is also partly double-counted in the source document: DoiT acquired SELECT and folded its technology into PerfectScale. SELECT ↗, DoiT acquisition announcement ↗
  • Migration components already exist. Lakebridge provides assessment, conversion, and reconciliation capabilities; SQLGlot provides parsing and transpilation. Their existence doesn't establish complete migration correctness. Lakebridge commands ↗, SQLGlot documentation ↗
  • Athena/Iceberg is a concrete target with constraints. Engine compatibility, catalog integration, supported operations, and timestamp precision need checking. S3 and catalog costs remain additional to query execution. Athena Iceberg documentation ↗, Athena pricing ↗

Plausible but unproven assumptions

  • A meaningful fraction of qualified customers have large savings left after native optimization.
  • Those savings concentrate in a repeatable class of portable workloads.
  • Metadata can identify that class efficiently.
  • Customers will authorize benchmarks, provide engineering time, and implement successful recommendations.
  • The same workflow recurs frequently enough to justify a subscription.
  • Human effort falls substantially across customers.
  • Outcome data generalizes enough to improve future assessments.
  • Customers permit the necessary collection and reuse of derived evidence.
  • A narrow AWS target wins enough deals to establish a business.

Speculation that should not influence the product yet

  • The "20% of workloads / 80% of addressable cost" distribution.
  • The document's precise compatibility and confidence percentages.
  • Its ACV ranges, willingness-to-pay scores, and venture scores.
  • Automatic cross-engine routing as the inevitable destination.
  • Enterprise-wide migration automation within a fixed timeline.
  • A proprietary cost model that reliably predicts every engine from query text.
  • The claim that no competitor owns workload-placement decisions.
  • "Multi-engine adoption will increase" as sufficient proof that customers want another platform.
The document contains no actual customer dataset, paid assessment, independently measured benchmark, or subscription renewal. Its numerical examples illustrate arguments; they do not validate them.

SECTION 4

Independent Startup Thesis

The strongest potential company, stated plainly.

The strongest potential company is a vendor-independent system that evaluates and governs data-pipeline changes against cost, correctness, and performance requirements, then verifies their realized economics.

The important distinction is the workflow: customers use it when introducing or changing meaningful workloads. They do not need to move existing pipelines between engines every month.

ElementProposed thesis
ProblemPlatform teams cannot cheaply establish whether a proposed optimization or offload will improve total economics without breaking outputs or service requirements. Consequently, they either avoid worthwhile changes or implement poorly substantiated ones.
ICPAWS-based companies with substantial Snowflake batch processing, dbt or similarly versioned pipelines, an existing approved AWS analytics environment, and regular pipeline changes. Start by testing roughly $1M–$5M annual Snowflake spend.

The venture case remains conditional. For illustration, $100M ARR requires 1,000 customers at $100K ARR or 500 at $200K. Merely listing additional supported engines does not establish either path.

SECTION 5

What Not to Build

Cut these aggressively.

  • A generic multi-cloud cost dashboard. Useful, established, and unlikely to win your first customers on differentiation.
  • A general Snowflake optimizer. Use existing tools and bounded expert tuning to establish the baseline.
  • A universal SQL translation copilot. Reuse parsers and translators; own the evidence that a specific transformation works.
  • A table-copying or schema-conversion company. Necessary plumbing with weak pricing power.
  • A new catalog or Iceberg maintenance service. Infrastructure vendors already provide substantial functionality, including managed maintenance. AWS S3 Tables maintenance ↗
  • A metadata-only "savings calculator" with precise dollar promises. Treat it as screening, with explicit unknowns.
  • A universal lineage platform. Build only the dependency evidence necessary to evaluate selected workloads.
  • A new distributed query engine or transparent SQL proxy. Introduces a much larger correctness, availability, and compatibility obligation.
  • Automatic real-time workload routing. Migration, data placement, policies, and operational ownership all have switching costs.
  • General CDC, dual writes, and automatic rollback. Can consume the company before demand is established.
  • Arbitrary stored-procedure and Snowpark modernization. Accepting every exception creates an unbounded services business.
  • Cold-data archival as the defining wedge. Infrequently accessed data may have little compute spend to remove; retained copies and access requirements may weaken storage savings.
  • "Savings guaranteed" pricing. Baselines, commitments, demand changes, and implementation responsibility make premature guarantees dangerous.
  • A large platform shell before a successful benchmark. Authentication, dashboards, and connector breadth do not answer the central business question.
Early manual work is acceptable when it teaches a repeatable product. Repeatedly accepting unrelated enterprise exceptions is a different business.
SECTION 6

The Wedge

A 30-day paid offload proof for one bounded Snowflake SQL pipeline.

Target pipeline criteria

  • Has a named owner and meaningful recurring compute cost.
  • Runs on an identifiable warehouse or schedule whose consumption can actually decline.
  • Uses partition-bounded SQL transformations.
  • Has a clear batch completion deadline.
  • Has few external dependencies.
  • Can obtain inputs economically from existing S3 data or a bounded export.
  • Has an approved path for delivering its outputs.

A pipeline that requires continuous full-table extraction from Snowflake is a poor first candidate, even if its SQL is portable. The 30-day period starts after the customer supplies the agreed access and names an engineer. Track the pre-access delay separately as part of sales economics.

PeriodWork and deliverable
Days 1–5Reconcile recent workload activity with spend, obtain contractual rates, identify feasible native improvements, and establish the customer's savings and performance criteria.
Days 6–10Select up to three candidates; map boundaries, owners, policies, freshness, and dependencies. Choose one for the benchmark.
Days 11–20Prepare a reproducible dataset, port the pipeline to one target, and run source-versus-target comparisons with a bounded tuning budget.
Days 21–30Validate outputs, test representative operating conditions, calculate conservative economics, and deliver an implementation-ready recommendation or a documented rejection.

An illustrative successful result (not a promised outcome)

"We identified $420K/year of potentially reducible Snowflake consumption across three bounded workloads. We benchmarked the strongest candidate. After feasible Snowflake optimization, that candidate has $300K/year of removable source cost. The target requires $140K–$170K/year including operation and data movement, implying $130K–$160K annual operating savings. With $50K of one-time transition cost, first-year benefit is $80K–$110K before our recurring fee. The tested runs meet the agreed output and completion requirements."

That conclusion must separately state when reductions become cash savings. If an existing commitment prevents immediate savings, report avoided future growth or renewal savings as such.

Automate immediately

  • Metadata import and reconciliation.
  • Query fingerprinting and candidate grouping.
  • A rules-based compatibility inventory.
  • Repeatable execution and result capture.
  • Output comparisons.
  • A versioned economic worksheet and evidence report.

Keep manual initially

  • Workload boundary and owner confirmation.
  • Contract interpretation with the customer.
  • Target layout and bounded tuning.
  • Limited SQL remediation.
  • Governance review.
  • Production integration planning and customer-operated cutover.
Do not call annual savings "verified" after a short benchmark. The benchmark measures a defined execution scenario; annual economics remain a projection until operating results support them.
SECTION 7

Technical Product Architecture

One customer-run container, customer-owned storage, a small analysis database, and a reproducible execution package. A multi-tenant SaaS control plane can wait.

Snowflake metadata ─────┐ ├──▶ Customer-run collector dbt artifacts + owner ──┘ │ ▼ Workload evidence & economics │ ▼ Selected benchmark plan │ ▼ Customer AWS runner │ │ ▼ ▼ Snowflake baseline Athena / Glue / S3 Iceberg │ │ └──────┬───────┘ ▼ Correctness & performance comparison │ ▼ Business case & implementation decision

Metadata collection

Collect roughly 60–90 days of detailed history initially, plus longer aggregate spend history to identify seasonality:

  • QUERY_HISTORY: SQL, hashes, tags, timings, scans, failures, and execution context.
  • Warehouse metering, load, and events.
  • Standard-warehouse query attribution.
  • Table sizes, schemas, view definitions, tasks, and policy references.
  • ACCESS_HISTORY where available.
  • dbt manifest and run results.
  • Customer-provided effective pricing and commitment information.

Handle Adaptive Warehouses separately: current documentation uses QUERY_METERING_HISTORY for their per-query consumption, with potentially multiple hourly records per query. Detect capabilities rather than assuming one collector query works everywhere. Adaptive query metering ↗

Workload identification

Group by dbt invocation/model, query tags, query fingerprints, parent-child execution relationships, schedules, and read/write sets. Require human confirmation for the selected workload. One query pattern may belong to multiple business processes.

Dependency graph

Store typed edges such as reads, writes, schedules, consumes, and protects. Attach provenance and observation time to every edge; distinguish observed, statically inferred, and owner-confirmed relationships.

OBJECT_DEPENDENCIES omits data movement through CTAS, INSERT, and MERGE, so it cannot independently establish pipeline lineage. Snowflake dependency limitations ↗

For the wedge, ordinary relational tables are sufficient. Unknown external consumers should block a production recommendation until resolved.

Cost attribution

Maintain three distinct values:

  1. Attributed consumption: workload allocation for discovery.
  2. Avoidable consumption: estimated reduction after removing or rescheduling it.
  3. Realizable financial benefit: effect after contracts, discounts, added target spend, and timing.

Reconcile usage against billed totals, explaining idle usage and other differences. Never multiply query duration by warehouse price and assume concurrent queries each consumed the entire warehouse. Warehouse metering documentation ↗

Portability analysis

Use SQL parsing, compiled dbt SQL, schema information, and a versioned capability matrix. Return: supported within the chosen scope, requires a known transformation, requires investigation, or blocked. Inspect types, timestamps, null behavior, semi-structured operations, incremental logic, policies, and external dependencies. A parseable query is not proof of equivalent behavior.

TCO estimation

Annual operating benefit = source expenditure actually avoidable − all incremental target operating costs.

First-year benefit = operating benefit during the actual post-cutover period − assessment, transition, dual-run, and product costs.

Include compute, storage, requests, catalog, data transfer, extraction, refresh, maintenance, orchestration, monitoring, and customer labor. Show low/base/high scenarios and identify which assumptions dominate. Do not count one-time migration cost both upfront and again as annual amortization in the same calculation.

Benchmarking

Choose Athena engine 3 + Glue catalog + Iceberg on S3, in the customer's AWS region, as the first supported configuration. For native Snowflake inputs, use an approved consistent snapshot/export into customer S3, followed by target-table creation. Existing compatible S3 inputs can avoid this step. A directory of exported Parquet files alone is not an Iceberg table.

Benchmark: identical logical inputs and required outputs; historical parameters and representative partition sizes; normal and peak operating conditions; relevant concurrency, queue time, retries, and completion time; cache behavior with result reuse documented and controlled; equal, explicitly bounded tuning effort.

Allow each engine an appropriate physical layout, but price the work needed to create and maintain it. Include large partitions, skew, and backfills — a small random sample cannot substantiate production-scale savings.

Validation

Compare schemas, row counts, keys, duplicates, null behavior, exact values where required, and agreed business metrics. Use canonicalized hashes or aggregates for scalable screening, followed by targeted differences. Apply tolerances only where the customer's semantics permit them — do not hide timestamp truncation or financial rounding behind broad numeric tolerances. Athena's documented timestamp precision limitation is a concrete example requiring explicit handling. Athena Iceberg constraints ↗

Customer-VPC / BYOC execution

Run the collector and orchestration container in the customer's environment, using separate metadata and benchmark roles, scoped credentials, encrypted storage, execution limits, and audit logs.

Athena compute is an AWS-managed service; BYOC does not mean its workers run inside the customer's VPC. Describe that boundary accurately. Keep raw SQL, literals, data, and detailed results customer-side by default. Export only approved derived evidence.

Where LLMs help

  • Explaining unsupported constructs.
  • Drafting bounded SQL changes.
  • Suggesting semantic edge cases.
  • Summarizing observed blockers and remediation work.

Where deterministic systems are required

  • Access control and execution scope.
  • Cost arithmetic and reconciliation.
  • Query identity and provenance.
  • Capability gates.
  • Benchmark measurement.
  • Result comparison and acceptance criteria.
An LLM must never invent dependencies, certify correctness, or generate a confidence percentage from narrative judgment.
SECTION 8

The Core Intelligence / Moat

The most valuable dataset is a corpus of tested workload changes and realized outcomes.

Each record should connect:

  • Source pipeline structure and dependency boundary.
  • Data characteristics: scale, skew, cardinality, partitioning, update pattern.
  • Engine versions and configurations.
  • Business requirements.
  • Proposed change and required remediation.
  • Predicted versus measured cost and performance.
  • Validation failures and their causes.
  • Engineering and operational effort.
  • Whether the customer implemented the change.
  • Realized consumption and financial outcomes.
  • Reasons a technically successful proposal was rejected.
Negative results are essential. A corpus containing only successful migrations will systematically overstate opportunity.

The feedback loop becomes valuable if each customer reduces the next customer's assessment effort, improves prediction calibration, or prevents a known failure.

Three constraints limit defensibility

  1. Data rights: permission to analyze a customer's SQL does not imply permission to reuse it for cross-customer learning.
  2. Generalization: benchmarks depend heavily on data distribution, configuration, and workload context.
  3. Drift: engine improvements and price changes age results.

Major vendors can reproduce parsers, runners, reports, and much of the predictive modeling. They may have vastly more execution telemetry. A neutral vendor can potentially accumulate better paired cross-platform intervention evidence, including rejected changes — but that advantage must be earned.

Realistic moat assessment: weak initially; potentially moderate with scale and workflow integration. The customer's operational dependency on your validated change process may become more defensible than a standalone prediction model.

Proposed gate before committing to substantial platform engineering

  • Three paid assessments.
  • At least two compelling cases that survive native optimization and full TCO.
  • One production offload with measured operating results.
  • Evidence of repeat paid use beyond the original assessment.
  • Delivery effort declining across customers without excluding most of the valuable opportunity.
These are learning gates, not statistical proof. Their purpose is to make expensive self-deception harder.
SECTION 11 · FINAL

Final Decision

Do more validation before building.

The technical components are feasible within a narrow scope. What remains unproven is whether enough customers have removable spend, will act on the evidence, and will pay repeatedly for a product that becomes cheaper to deliver over time.

Build only the tooling necessary to answer those questions. The document does not yet justify a broad workload platform.

A. The company, in one sentence

A vendor-independent system that helps data teams approve economically worthwhile pipeline changes and verify their production outcomes.

B. The first product, in one sentence

A paid, reproducible benchmark that determines whether one bounded Snowflake pipeline should move to Athena/Iceberg after accounting for correctness, performance, and full economics.

C. The first customer profile, in one sentence

An AWS-based company spending roughly $1M–$5M annually on Snowflake, with costly batch SQL pipelines, an approved AWS analytics environment, and an accountable owner under a concrete cost mandate.

D. The first thing to build in Codex

A customer-run command-line analyzer that imports Snowflake metadata and dbt artifacts, reconciles spend, and produces an auditable shortlist distinguishing attributed cost, potentially avoidable cost, dependencies, and unresolved assumptions.

E. The first thing NOT to build

A multi-engine dashboard with automated migration or workload routing.

F. The single most urgent question for a real customer

"Which workload will you actually authorize us to offload this quarter if we prove it meets your requirements, and how much spend must disappear from your budget to justify doing it?"
